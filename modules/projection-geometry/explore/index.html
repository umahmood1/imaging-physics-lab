Projection Geometry and Detector Signal

Trace rays from a point source through selectable attenuating objects and a collimated field to a flat detector. See how distance, angle, and the material in the way set the signal at each point of the detector. The Parallel beam tab does the same for a beam of parallel rays. The model uses one X-ray energy.

Introduction. This is a model of how a radiograph forms: an X-ray source, a collimator, an object, and a flat detector. Use it to see why one point on the detector receives more or less signal than another. Four things decide it: how far the ray travels, the angle at which it strikes the detector, how much material it passes through, and whether the collimator lets it through.

How to use this page

  1. Pick a point. Click the ray picture or the detector face. The point you pick, P, is marked in all three pictures.
  2. Read the result. The dark strip below gives the relative signal at P, the length of material the ray crosses, and the three factors that multiply to give that signal.
  3. Change the experiment. Drag the object, set distances and materials under “Experiment setup”, or load a textbook problem with “Scenario”.

Detector sharpness (not applied to the pictures): pixels 0.15 mm apart, blur 0.30 mm wide. About 70% of the contrast of 1 line pair per mm is kept.

Calculation and limits

Source to skin = SOD − thickness/2 = 44.0 cm. Incident air kerma = tube output × mAs × a factor for the tube voltage, (kVp/120)² × any change to the beam set in the comparison panel × (100 cm / source-to-skin distance)². Entrance surface air kerma includes backscatter. Detector air kerma uses inverse square to SID and exp(−μ × thickness). DI = 10 log₁₀(detector kerma / target).

The 22 cm thickness, 0.05 mGy/mAs output, 0.14 cm⁻¹ effective attenuation, backscatter factor, and receptor targets are editable teaching assumptions, not measured case values or clinical recommendations. A PA or AP image alone does not establish patient thickness; no paired calibrated lateral view is available for these cases. The homogeneous path estimate omits the distinct lung and bone paths, scatter at the receptor, automatic exposure control, and measured beam calibration. The MTF estimate combines a Gaussian blur with a square-pixel aperture.

Sources: AAPM Task Group 116 (the deviation index); IAEA, Diagnostic Radiology Physics.
