CT Artifacts: What You See, the Cause, and the Fix

An artifact is something in the image that is not in the patient. Each case below shows one artifact in a chest image, beside the fault in the scanner’s data that causes it. The cases are grouped by what the artifact looks like, as the textbooks group them: streaks, and rings and bands. Streaks have many causes, so each cause has its own case. For each you get what a reader sees, the cause, the physics that gives it its shape, and the fix. The scanner these faults come from →

  1. Left: the detector data. One reading is one detector’s measurement at one position of the X-ray tube. A slice is computed from about 870,000 of them: 984 tube positions (views), 888 detectors each. The faulty readings are marked.
  2. Right: the image. Switch between the patient with the artifact, the artifact alone, and the same slice from a fault-free scan.
  3. Beside them: the artifact’s name in the textbooks, what you see, the cause, and the fix, with one control to try.
  4. Under them: the physics. One equation from the reconstruction says why the artifact has its shape.
What the symbols mean
g = −ln(I/I₀)
What one reading says: how strongly the patient attenuated that ray. I photons were counted with the patient in the beam, I₀ without. The image is computed from these numbers.
ε
The error in g of a wrong reading. A reading that is 1% low gives ε = −ln(0.99) ≈ 0.01.
ℓ
The distance of a detector’s ray from the isocenter (the center of rotation), in cm. It is the same in every view.
β
The angle of the tube around the patient. One view is one tube angle; a full turn has 984 views. At β = 0° the tube is at 12 o’clock, above the patient.
c̃
The reconstruction filter (a ramp filter with a smoothing kernel). Each row of readings is filtered with it before being laid along the rays.
HU
Hounsfield units, the scale of CT numbers: water is 0 and air −1000. One HU is a thousandth of water’s attenuation.
P
A point you choose in the image; the line under the pictures gives the CT number there.
W, L
Width and level of the gray scale: CT numbers from L − W/2 to L + W/2 are shown from black to white.

The physics: why it has this shape

Δμ(x,y)=one view’s shareNπT​​​k∑​error of reading kεk​​​c~(xcosβk​+ysinβk​−ℓk​)

This is the reconstruction sum (Prince and Links, Eq. 6.61, written for the N = 984 views of a full turn), applied to the errors alone. Reconstruction filters every reading with c̃ and lays it along its own ray, the line x cos βk + y sin βk = ℓk, because nothing in a reading says where along the ray the attenuation was. c̃ is positive on the ray and negative just beside it, so a reading that is wrong by εk adds a bright line with dark edges, or the reverse. In a fault-free scan each line is cancelled by its neighbors and by the other views. An error has no partner, so it stays: that is a streak. All the rays of one view leave the same focal spot, so the streaks of a spoiled view meet at the tube. T is the detector spacing, 0.56 mm. Each εk is noise of about one over the square root of the photons counted, so the rays through the most tissue make the strongest streaks. With the tube at 0.1% of its output, the ray through the most tissue counts about 11 photons where a normal view counts about 10,800.

What you see
A fan of thin straight streaks, bright and dark side by side. Followed back, they meet at one point outside the patient: where the X-ray tube was.
Cause
The X-ray tube, not a detector. A short circuit inside the tube (an arc) makes the power supply switch it off for a few milliseconds. For one view, or a few, almost no X-rays reach the patient, and every reading of that view is mostly noise.
The fix
The power supply detects the arc. The scanner discards the spoiled view and rebuilds it from the views on either side (try it under “More controls”). Frequent arcing means the tube is near the end of its life.
Try this

Move the tube to another position. Follow the streaks back: where do they meet? Then switch to “Artifact alone”.

Where the tube was when it arced
Worth noticing

The streaks point at the tube, so they tell a service engineer where it was when it arced. This also separates an arc from a faulty detector. A detector is wrong in every view and draws a circle around the isocenter. An arc spoils every detector in one view and draws straight lines from one point. One spoiled view in 984 is enough to see, because its errors are far larger than the noise of a normal view. A normal view’s noise is left out of this model.

Appearances and causes follow J. Hsieh, Computed Tomography: Principles, Design, Artifacts, and Recent Advances, 3rd ed. (SPIE Press), Sections 7.2 (streaking, ring and band artifacts, the center smudge), 7.3.1 (view aliasing), 7.3.4 (noise-induced streaks), 7.4.2 (tube arcing), 7.5.1 (detector calibration), and 7.6.1 (patient motion); the artifact names are the ones used there. The physics is written with the reconstruction equations of J. L. Prince and J. M. Links, Medical Imaging Signals and Systems, 2nd ed., Sections 6.3.3, 6.4.2, and 6.4.3. Every picture and number is computed here. The model is simplified: one X-ray energy, noise only in the tube-arcing and photon-starvation cases, and parallel rays except in the view spoiled by tube arcing, which uses the scanner’s fan of rays. Breathing is a steady rise of the front of the chest at 5 mm per second, an assumed typical value. Streaks also come from beam hardening, metal, partial volume, too few detectors (detector aliasing), and a patient who reaches outside the scan field; those causes are not shown yet, and the first two need more than one X-ray energy. The patient is a chest made of ellipses, not a scan of a person.
