A CT Scanner, from the Tube to the Image

A CT scanner sends X-rays through a patient from every direction, measures how much gets through, and computes a picture of a slice from those measurements. This page is a working model of one. Change or remove any part and see what happens to the picture. The mathematics of reconstruction →

  1. The scanner (left). The X-ray tube circles the object, and the detector opposite it counts the photons that get through.
  2. The image (middle) is computed from one full turn. It shows the object’s slice in CT numbers: water 0 HU, air −1000 HU.
  3. The difference (right) shows only what your changes did to the image. It is blank until you change something.
  4. Start with part 1, the tube. Each part has one question, one equation, and one thing to try. The detector’s raw data are at the bottom.
What the symbols mean
I₀
The number of photons a detector counts when nothing is in the beam.
I
The number it counts with the object in the beam.
g = −ln(I/I₀)
How strongly the object attenuated that ray. The image is computed from these numbers.
β
The angle of the tube around the object: 0° with the tube at the top of the drawing, increasing counterclockwise.
γ
The angle of one ray within the fan, measured from the middle ray.
P
A point you choose and follow through every picture. The circle around it is where the CT number and the noise are measured.
HU
Hounsfield units, the scale of CT numbers: 1000 × (μ − μwater)/μwater.
W, L
Width and level of the gray-scale window: CT numbers from L − W/2 to L + W/2 are shown from black to white.
kV, mA
The tube’s voltage, which sets the photons’ energies, and its current, which sets how many there are.
Beam shape

A fan of X-rays and an arc of detectors read every ray of a view at once. Slip rings (1987) let the tube turn without stopping, and spiral scanning followed in 1989: a slice in a second or less. The fan’s readings are re-sorted into parallel rays before reconstruction.

At 120 kV on the large focal spot the tube can draw up to 835 mA: that is the 100 kW the focal spot can take. One turn puts 18.0 kJ into the anode, nearly all of it as heat, and a 10 s scan puts in 360 kJ.

Close-up: what does the beam look like at each voltage?

Each curve is the beam after the filters at one voltage, for the same mAs; the highest value at 140 kV is set to 100. No photon can carry more energy than the tube voltage gives an electron, so each curve ends at its kV. A higher voltage makes more photons per mAs and more energetic ones, which cross the patient more easily.

The curves follow Kramers’ smooth model; a real tungsten anode adds sharp lines near 59 and 69 keV. Values on this page are representative of a 64-slice scanner of this type, not a manufacturer’s specification.

Why does the focal spot limit the current?

Electrons hit the anode on a small patch, the focal spot. About 99% of their energy turns into heat there. A smaller spot gives a slightly sharper image (on this page too: compare the difference image), but the same power on a smaller patch would melt the track. The scanner therefore allows about 40 kW on the small spot and 100 kW on the large one. At 80 and 100 kV the limit is different: the filament cannot push more current across the tube at such a low voltage.

A CT tube stays on for seconds at hundreds of milliamps while it circles the patient two or three times a second. A radiography tube fires for a few milliseconds. So a CT tube is built around heat, and it offers only four voltages, because every calibration the scanner stores is measured at each one.

From the detector’s readings to the image

In one turn the tube stops at 984 positions, and at each one the 888 detectors of the slice count photons. Six steps turn that table of 984 × 888 numbers into the image above. Follow them in order: each shows one view as a graph and, under it, all the views as a picture.

1One view: every detector counts the photons that reach it

The tube stands at β = 0°. In this one view each of the 888 detectors counts the photons that reach it: Id. With nothing in the beam the same detector would count I0. The graph shows this one view. The picture under it holds every view of the turn, and this view is one row of it.

What if a detector fails?

Every detector is working. A faulty detector is one column of the data; a lost view is one row.

The Standard kernel: sharpness and noise texture
  • Contrast kept in the image (modulation transfer function, MTF)
  • Noise power (noise power spectrum, NPS)

Solid: the share of an object’s contrast that reaches the image at each level of detail, for the focal spot in use (50% at 4.2 and 10% at 6.8 line pairs per cm with the small spot, typical of a Standard body kernel). The width of the detector cells and of the focal spot blur detail first; the kernel lifts it back. Dashed: the noise power spectrum. Noise is not blurred by the hardware, so it gets the kernel’s lift in full and peaks at 2.7 line pairs per cm, which gives it its grain. Images are 512 × 512. Only this kernel and filtered backprojection are modeled; the scatter correction removes 90% of the scatter.
