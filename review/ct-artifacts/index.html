CT Artifacts: What to Recognize and What to Do

Eight artifacts in the order of the table. For each: what you see, why it happens, what the technologist can do, what the radiologist should know, and the points to take home. See each one form in the lab →

The eight artifacts at a glance

First question for any artifact: is it centered on the middle of the scan field? Rings, bands, and a center smudge are, and they point to the scanner. Streaks usually are not.

1Tube arcing

Thin streaks cross one slice, and all of them point to one spot outside the body. What went wrong?

What you see
Fine straight streaks, bright and dark side by side, across one slice. Followed back, they meet at one point outside the body: where the X-ray tube was at that moment. The slices before and after look normal.
Why it happens
An arc is a brief electrical short inside the X-ray tube. It cuts the X-ray output for a few milliseconds, so one of the roughly 1,000 views taken in a rotation gets almost no X-rays. Every reading in that view is mostly noise, and the reconstruction spreads each one along its ray. All the rays of one view leave the tube’s focal spot, so the streaks point back to it.
What the technologist can do
Nothing in the technique causes it. Scanners detect an arc and replace the spoiled view with one made from the views on either side, so an arc that shows in an image got past that correction. Record the scanner’s error message, tell the radiologist which images have the streaks, and call service. Whether to rescan is the radiologist’s decision.
What the radiologist should know
Streaks on a single image that converge on one point outside the body come from the equipment, not from the anatomy.

Take-home points

  • One bad view makes streaks that point at the X-ray tube.
  • An arc that shows in an image is a service call.

2Photon starvation

Streaks run straight across the body from side to side, between the arms. Why there?

What you see
Fine bright and dark streaks running side to side at the level of the arms or shoulders, with grainy noise in that band. Above and below it the image looks normal.
Why it happens
From the side, the X-rays must cross both arms and the whole body. So few get through that those readings are mostly noise, and the reconstruction spreads the noise along those rays. This is called photon starvation.
What the technologist can do
Raise the patient’s arms above the head when the exam allows. Use tube current modulation, which raises the tube current for the side views, where the body is widest. Raising the dose for the whole scan helps less: in this phantom, doubling it lowers the noise of the worst ray by about 40%.
What the radiologist should know
Streaks that run along the widest path through the body, usually at the shoulders, arms, or hips, are noise, not structure. Scanner software that smooths the noisiest readings (adaptive filtering) reduces them.

Take-home points

  • The streaks follow the path with the most tissue in it.
  • Arms up and tube current modulation prevent them better than more dose.

3Motion

Edges look doubled, and streaks leave them. What moved?

What you see
Blurred or doubled edges, streaks leaving the edges that moved, and shading in soft tissue. In the chest, breathing doubles the edges of the chest wall, the heart, and the lungs.
Why it happens
Reconstruction assumes the body is still for the whole rotation. If it moves, the views from the start and the end of the rotation, taken from almost the same place, disagree, and no single image fits all the views.
What the technologist can do
Give clear breath-hold instructions, use positioning aids to keep the patient still, and use the shortest scan time the protocol allows. For the heart, use ECG gating.
What the radiologist should know
Heart motion can double the wall of the aorta so that it looks like a dissection (Barrett and Keat, 2004).

Take-home points

  • Motion makes the first and last views of a rotation disagree.
  • Less time per rotation means less motion during it.

4View aliasing

Fine streaks begin a short way out from a small dense object. Why not right at it?

What you see
Fine, regular streaks fanning out from small dense objects and sharp edges. Close to the object the image is clean; the streaks begin some distance away and grow.
Why it happens
The scanner took too few views per rotation for the detail the image keeps. Next to the object, the lines from neighboring views lie close together and blend. Farther out they spread apart and show as separate streaks. This is called view aliasing.
What the technologist can do
The number of views is set by the scanner, not by the technique. If the scanner offers a mode with more views per rotation, use it. A smoother reconstruction kernel makes the streaks fainter and the image less sharp.
What the radiologist should know
Fine regular streaks that start away from a dense object are a sampling artifact, not a finding.

Take-home points

  • The streaks start away from the object, not at it.
  • Clinical scanners take about 1,000 views per rotation to keep them out of the image.

5Ring artifact

A thin circle sits in the image, centered on the middle of the scanner, not on the body. Where does it come from?

What you see
A thin bright or dark circle, or part of one, centered on the center of the scan field. It crosses organs without following any of them.
Why it happens
One detector element is out of calibration and reads slightly wrong at every tube position. The detector turns with the tube, so in every view the error falls at the same distance from the center: a circle.
What the technologist can do
Run the scanner’s calibration as the manufacturer specifies. If the ring comes back, call service: the detector may need to be replaced.
What the radiologist should know
A circle centered on the middle of the scan field that ignores the anatomy is a ring artifact.

Take-home points

  • The same error in every view makes a ring.
  • The ring’s radius is the faulty detector’s distance from the center of the detector.

6Center smudge

A small spot sits exactly in the middle of the scan field. Lesion or artifact?

What you see
A small bright or dark spot, or a tiny ring, at the exact center of the scan field, in the same place on every slice. It can look like a lesion.
Why it happens
The same calibration error as a ring, in a detector at the middle of the array. Errors in the central detectors are amplified the most, so a small error makes a strong spot.
What the technologist can do
Run the calibration, then scan the water phantom and look at the center of the image. If the spot is still there, call service.
What the radiologist should know
A spot at the exact center of the scan field, in the same place on consecutive slices, points to a central detector; Barrett and Keat describe it as a dark smudge at the center of the image.

Take-home points

  • Faults in the central detectors are amplified the most.
  • At the center of the field and on every slice: suspect the scanner.

7Band artifact

A broad ring of slightly different density surrounds the center. Is it real?

What you see
A wide, soft-edged ring in which CT numbers are a little too high or too low. It has no sharp edge, so it is easy to miss or to take for real density.
Why it happens
Several neighboring detectors have drifted together since calibration. A smooth error like this makes a soft band instead of a sharp ring.
What the technologist can do
Bands are found on the daily water phantom scan viewed with a narrow window: the water should be flat. Recalibrate, and call service if the band stays.
What the radiologist should know
A subtle change in density that forms part of a circle around the center of the field and ignores organ boundaries can be a band artifact. It shifts CT numbers measured inside it.

Take-home points

  • A smooth error makes a soft band, which is harder to spot than a ring.
  • The daily water phantom scan is where bands are found.

8Failed detector

A very bright ring with streaks, and wrong CT numbers inside it. What failed?

What you see
A ring far brighter than bone, streaks leaving it, and CT numbers shifted everywhere inside it.
Why it happens
One detector element stopped working. It reports no X-rays at all, which the scanner takes to mean total attenuation at every tube position.
What the technologist can do
Scanners keep a list of dead detectors and fill in their readings from the neighbors, so this should not reach an image. If it does, stop and call service.
What the radiologist should know
Do not measure CT numbers in an image like this: inside the ring they are all wrong.

Take-home points

  • A dead detector makes a ring with streaks.
  • Filling in its readings from the neighbors hides it almost completely.
